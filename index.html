رحلة العمرة

What Umrah is, and the journey it follows

Umrah means "to visit a populated place" — the lesser pilgrimage to the Ka'bah, performed any time of year rather than on Hajj's fixed dates. Its rites trace back to Ibrahim and Isma'il, peace be upon them, who raised the Ka'bah and whose family's search for water became the walk between Safa and Marwah; the Prophet ﷺ himself performed it four times.

"Umrah after Umrah is an expiation for what came between them."

Sahih al-Bukhari & Sahih Muslim

"Follow up Hajj and Umrah, for they remove poverty and sin as the bellows removes impurity from iron."

Sunan al-Tirmidhi

Scholars differ on whether Umrah is obligatory once in a lifetime — the Shafi'i and Hanbali view, reading Qur'an 2:196 — or a strongly emphasised sunnah, as the Hanafi and Maliki view holds. Either way, it's one of the most rewarded acts of worship a Muslim can undertake. Whatever the ruling on obligation, the rites themselves are fixed — four pillars, in sequence — and the next section opens each one with its exact wording.

١

Ihram

الإحرام

At or before the miqat: ghusl, the ihram garments, the intention, and the Talbiyah — the state you enter and stay in until the final trim.

٢

Tawaf

الطواف

Seven circuits of the Ka'bah beginning at the Black Stone (Hajar al-Aswad), then two rak'ahs near Maqam Ibrahim and a drink of Zamzam.

٣

Sa'i

السعي

Seven passes between Safa and Marwah, retracing Hajar's search for water — the walk that gave Zamzam its name.

٤

Halq or Taqsir

الحلق أو التقصير

Shaving or trimming the hair closes Umrah and releases you from ihram — the visit is complete.

الأدعية

Every step, with its wording

Each stage below has its Arabic text, transliteration and English meaning — tap to open it, then press Listen to hear it spoken aloud.

Perform ghusl — a full purifying bath, the Prophet's ﷺ own practice before entering ihram — then wear the ihram garments (unstitched white cloths for men; plain, modest clothing for women — the face and hands remain uncovered), make your intention, and begin reciting the Talbiyah, which you repeat often until you start Tawaf.

Intention (niyyah) for Umrah

اللَّهُمَّ إِنِّي أُرِيدُ الْعُمْرَةَ فَيَسِّرْهَا لِي وَتَقَبَّلْهَا مِنِّي

Allāhumma innī urīdul-'umrata fa yassirhā lī wa taqabbalhā minnī

O Allah, I intend to perform Umrah — make it easy for me, and accept it from me.

Intention is an act of the heart; this wording is a commonly taught form, not a fixed recitation.

The Talbiyah

لَبَّيْكَ اللَّهُمَّ لَبَّيْكَ، لَبَّيْكَ لَا شَرِيكَ لَكَ لَبَّيْكَ، إِنَّ الْحَمْدَ وَالنِّعْمَةَ لَكَ وَالْمُلْكَ، لَا شَرِيكَ لَكَ

Labbayk Allāhumma labbayk, labbayka lā sharīka laka labbayk, innal-ḥamda wan-ni'mata laka wal-mulk, lā sharīka lak

Here I am, O Allah, here I am. Here I am, You have no partner, here I am. Verily all praise, grace and dominion are Yours. You have no partner.

Sahih al-Bukhari & Sahih Muslim. Men recite it aloud; women say it quietly.

Enter with the right foot, as with any mosque, and lower your gaze in humility as the Ka'bah comes into view. Many raise their hands and make personal du'a at first sight — there's no single wording required for that moment; speak to Allah in your own words.

Entering the mosque

اللَّهُمَّ افْتَحْ لِي أَبْوَابَ رَحْمَتِكَ

Allāhumma-ftaḥ lī abwāba raḥmatik

O Allah, open the gates of Your mercy for me.

Sahih Muslim — the general dua for entering any mosque.

Begin level with the Black Stone (Hajar al-Aswad), Ka'bah on your left. Say the takbir at the start of each of the seven circuits — kissing or touching the stone if possible, otherwise, gesture toward it without pushing. Between the Yemeni Corner and the Black Stone (Hajar al-Aswad), recite the verse below; elsewhere, any dhikr or personal du'a is fine. Close with two rak'ahs near Maqam Ibrahim (Surah al-Kafirun, then al-Ikhlas) and a drink of Zamzam.

Starting each circuit

بِسْمِ اللَّهِ، وَاللَّهُ أَكْبَرُ

Bismillāh, wallāhu akbar

In the name of Allah, and Allah is the Greatest.

Between the Yemeni Corner and the Black Stone (Hajar al-Aswad)

رَبَّنَا آتِنَا فِي الدُّنْيَا حَسَنَةً وَفِي الْآخِرَةِ حَسَنَةً وَقِنَا عَذَابَ النَّارِ

Rabbanā ātinā fid-dunyā ḥasanatan wa fil-ākhirati ḥasanatan wa qinā 'adhāban-nār

Our Lord, give us good in this world and good in the Hereafter, and protect us from the punishment of the Fire.

Qur'an 2:201, repeated by the Prophet ﷺ on this exact stretch of Tawaf (Sunan Abu Dawud).

After Tawaf, at Maqam Ibrahim

وَاتَّخِذُوا مِنْ مَقَامِ إِبْرَاهِيمَ مُصَلًّى

Wattakhidhū min maqāmi Ibrāhīma muṣallā

And take the station of Ibrahim as a place of prayer.

Qur'an 2:125 — recited before the two rak'ahs. Pray them anywhere in the mosque if the area is crowded.

Approach Safa reciting the verse below, then climb it, face the Ka'bah, and say the takbir three times followed by the dhikr — repeating the whole sequence three times with your own du'a in between. Walk to Marwah (this is one circuit), repeat the same dhikr there, and continue for seven one-way trips in total, ending at Marwah.

Approaching Safa

إِنَّ الصَّفَا وَالْمَرْوَةَ مِنْ شَعَائِرِ اللَّهِ ﴿١٥٨﴾ أَبْدَأُ بِمَا بَدَأَ اللَّهُ بِهِ

Innaṣ-ṣafā wal-marwata min sha'ā'irillāh — abda'u bimā bada'a Allāhu bih

Indeed, Safa and Marwah are among the symbols of Allah. I begin with what Allah began with.

Qur'an 2:158, with the Prophet's ﷺ own opening words (Sahih Muslim).

On Safa and on Marwah

لَا إِلَهَ إِلَّا اللَّهُ وَحْدَهُ لَا شَرِيكَ لَهُ، لَهُ الْمُلْكُ وَلَهُ الْحَمْدُ يُحْيِي وَيُمِيتُ وَهُوَ عَلَى كُلِّ شَيْءٍ قَدِيرٌ، لَا إِلَهَ إِلَّا اللَّهُ وَحْدَهُ، أَنْجَزَ وَعْدَهُ وَنَصَرَ عَبْدَهُ وَهَزَمَ الْأَحْزَابَ وَحْدَهُ

Lā ilāha illallāhu waḥdahu lā sharīka lah, lahul-mulku wa lahul-ḥamdu yuḥyī wa yumītu wa huwa 'alā kulli shay'in qadīr. Lā ilāha illallāhu waḥdah, anjaza wa'dahu wa naṣara 'abdahu wa hazamal-aḥzāba waḥdah

There is no god but Allah alone, with no partner. His is the dominion and His is all praise; He gives life and causes death, and He has power over all things. There is no god but Allah alone, who fulfilled His promise, granted victory to His servant, and defeated the enemy alliance alone.

Sahih Muslim — said three times, facing the Qiblah, with personal du'a after each repetition.

Drinking Zamzam

اللَّهُمَّ إِنِّي أَسْأَلُكَ عِلْمًا نَافِعًا، وَرِزْقًا وَاسِعًا، وَشِفَاءً مِنْ كُلِّ دَاءٍ

Allāhumma innī as'aluka 'ilman nāfi'an, wa rizqan wāsi'an, wa shifā'an min kulli dā'

O Allah, I ask You for beneficial knowledge, ample provision, and healing from every illness.

A widely used form drawn on the hadith "Zamzam water is for whatever it is drunk for" (Ibn Majah) — make it your own du'a, this need not be recited verbatim.

Men shave the head completely (halq) or trim evenly all over (taqsir); the Prophet ﷺ prayed for those who shave three times and for those who trim once — shaving is the more complete option for men. Women cut roughly a fingertip's length from a gathered lock of hair. Once done, ihram restrictions end and your Umrah is complete — no fixed du'a is prescribed for this step itself.

What about Tawaf al-Wada' (the farewell tawaf)? It's obligatory before leaving Makkah after Hajj, but for Umrah alone most scholars consider it recommended rather than required — Umrah's own rites end at halq or taqsir. Many people still perform it as a graceful way to say goodbye to the Ka'bah before travelling home; skipping it after Umrah isn't missing a pillar.
A note on authenticity. Every Arabic text above comes from the Qur'an or from hadith in Sahih al-Bukhari, Sahih Muslim, Sunan Abu Dawud or Sunan Ibn Majah, referenced beside each one. Where scholars differ on wording or where no fixed text exists, that's noted rather than papered over. This page is a study companion, not a fatwa — for rulings on your specific situation, ask a qualified local scholar.

These aren't part of the four pillars above, but they're some of the most-read du'as around an Umrah trip — when you set out, when you first reach Madinah, and when you stand before the Prophet's ﷺ grave.

Setting out on the journey

اللَّهُ أَكْبَرُ اللَّهُ أَكْبَرُ اللَّهُ أَكْبَرُ، سُبْحَانَ الَّذِي سَخَّرَ لَنَا هَذَا وَمَا كُنَّا لَهُ مُقْرِنِينَ وَإِنَّا إِلَى رَبِّنَا لَمُنْقَلِبُونَ، اللَّهُمَّ إِنَّا نَسْأَلُكَ فِي سَفَرِنَا هَذَا الْبِرَّ وَالتَّقْوَى وَمِنَ الْعَمَلِ مَا تَرْضَى

Allāhu akbar, Allāhu akbar, Allāhu akbar. Subḥānal-ladhī sakhkhara lanā hādhā wa mā kunnā lahu muqrinīn, wa innā ilā rabbinā lamunqalibūn. Allāhumma innā nas'aluka fī safarinā hādhal-birra wat-taqwā, wa minal-'amali mā tarḍā

Allah is the Greatest, three times. Glory to the One who made this possible for us — we could not have done it ourselves — and to our Lord we shall return. O Allah, we ask You for righteousness and piety on this journey, and for deeds that please You.

Sahih Muslim, narrated by Ibn Umar — said whenever mounting transport to travel, including on the way to the airport.

Arriving in a new town (Madinah included)

اللَّهُمَّ رَبَّ السَّمَاوَاتِ السَّبْعِ وَمَا أَظْلَلْنَ، وَرَبَّ الْأَرَضِينَ السَّبْعِ وَمَا أَقْلَلْنَ، أَسْأَلُكَ خَيْرَ هَذِهِ الْقَرْيَةِ وَخَيْرَ أَهْلِهَا، وَأَعُوذُ بِكَ مِنْ شَرِّهَا وَشَرِّ أَهْلِهَا

Allāhumma rabbas-samāwātis-sab'i wa mā aẓlalna, wa rabbal-araḍīnas-sab'i wa mā aqlalna, as'aluka khayra hādhihil-qaryati wa khayra ahlihā, wa a'ūdhu bika min sharrihā wa sharri ahlihā

O Allah, Lord of the seven heavens and what they shade, and Lord of the seven earths and what they carry — I ask You for the good of this town and the good of its people, and I seek refuge in You from its evil and the evil of its people.

Sunan al-Nasa'i, graded hasan by several scholars — recited entering any town, and often said arriving in Madinah specifically.

Standing before the Prophet's ﷺ grave (in the Rawdah)

السَّلَامُ عَلَيْكَ أَيُّهَا النَّبِيُّ وَرَحْمَةُ اللَّهِ وَبَرَكَاتُهُ

As-salāmu 'alayka ayyuhan-nabiyyu wa raḥmatullāhi wa barakātuh

Peace be upon you, O Prophet, and the mercy of Allah and His blessings.

The same greeting said in every prayer's tashahhud (Sahih al-Bukhari & Sahih Muslim) — customarily repeated quietly, facing the grave, without touching or pressing against the grille.

At the Multazam (between the Black Stone (Hajar al-Aswad) and the door)

اللَّهُمَّ يَا رَبَّ الْبَيْتِ الْعَتِيقِ أَعْتِقْ رِقَابَنَا وَرِقَابَ آبَائِنَا وَأُمَّهَاتِنَا وَإِخْوَانِنَا وَأَوْلَادِنَا مِنَ النَّارِ

Allāhumma yā rabbal-baytil-'atīq, a'tiq riqābanā wa riqāba ābā'inā wa ummahātinā wa ikhwāninā wa awlādinā minan-nār

O Allah, Lord of the Ancient House, free our necks and the necks of our parents, siblings and children from the Fire.

Widely recited at the Multazam, though its chain is considered weak by several hadith scholars — treat it as a heartfelt personal supplication rather than a verified prophetic text, and feel free to make du'a here in your own words instead.

الصدقة

Making the most of sadaqah on this trip

Charity is rewarded everywhere, but many scholars hold that good deeds carry extra weight in the two Harams — reason enough for sadaqah to be a deliberate part of the trip, not an afterthought. The Prophet ﷺ said, "Charity does not decrease wealth" (Sahih Muslim), and "Save yourself from the Fire even with half a date given in charity" (Sahih al-Bukhari & Sahih Muslim).

Before you fly

Set a budget

Decide a fixed amount in advance, and settle any zakat or debts first — sadaqah doesn't substitute for those.

While there

Feed someone nearby

Dates, Zamzam or a meal at iftar is a loved local custom — give at organised points rather than in a crowd.

Lasting reward

Make it ongoing

A Zamzam cooler or Qur'an copies keep earning reward after you've left — mosque waqf offices accept this.

Give through trusted channels. Donation boxes inside both mosques, mosque waqf offices, and Saudi Arabia's Ehsan platform are reliable ways to make sure it reaches its cause — some street solicitation around the Haram is organised begging, not need.

Staying connected & managing money

Data for Nusuk and maps, and cash for everything else — the practical basics, in brief.

Getting online

Sawa Ziyara

~SAR 35, reservable online; fingerprint activation on arrival.

MBL

Mobily visitor pack

~SAR 30, walk-up at branches, airport kiosks or shops.

~SAR 34.5, kiosks at both Jeddah and Madinah airports.

Clustered near both Harams; look for official SAMA licensing signage.

International cards

Widely accepted; ATMs often beat counters once spread is factored in.

SAR is pegged to the USD at 3.75 and has been since the 1980s, so an "off" rate is just the exchanger's fee, not a market move. Travelling from India, BookMyForex, Wise or Thomas Cook India let you lock in Riyal cash or a forex card before you fly — compare a couple, rates vary. At any ATM, always choose to be charged in local currency (SAR), never "your home currency" (Dynamic Currency Conversion quietly costs more) — and stick to licensed exchangers only.

Seasons, weather, and why timing matters

Umrah has no fixed date on the solar calendar — the Islamic year is lunar, so your trip's Gregorian date shifts by about 10–11 days earlier each year. That means weather and crowd size are two separate things to plan around, and they don't always move together.

Jun–Sep

Summer

42–45°C+, dry heat, little midday shade — but fewer visitors, easier permits and hotels.

Dec–Feb

Winter

A comfortable 25–30°C — and the most crowded stretch of the year for exactly that reason.

Mar–May, Oct–Nov

Spring & autumn

30–38°C, occasional shoulder-season rain near Jeddah — often the best weather/crowd balance.

Two calendars to watch: Ramadan is the busiest stretch regardless of weather — book early, as Nusuk/Rawdah slots tighten to 3–5 days out. Umrah pauses entirely from 1 Dhul Qa'dah–13 Dhul Hijjah for Hajj (expected ~late May 2026, drifting earlier each year) — check current Hijri dates before planning around it. If your trip lands in summer, lean on the packing list's sun/hydration items and do Tawaf/Sa'i in the cooler early morning or post-Isha hours.

Getting around

Between cities, from the airport, and around the Haram itself — here's how pilgrims usually move.

CRM

Careem

  • The most widely used ride-hailing app in Saudi Arabia, with strong coverage in both Makkah and Madinah
  • Works well for airport pickups, city rides, and trips out to ziyarat sites
  • Account verification by SMS or WhatsApp — set it up before you land if you can
UBR

Uber

  • A solid alternative to Careem, with an account that usually carries over from home
  • Good for airport transfers — use the marked pickup zone and match the plate before getting in
  • Coverage and wait times can vary more than Careem during peak pilgrimage weeks
JNY

Jeeny

  • A local Saudi ride app that can undercut Careem or Uber on price in Makkah and Madinah
  • Some foreign phone numbers can't register — worth trying only once you have a Saudi SIM
  • Useful as a backup when the other two are short on drivers
1

Between Jeddah, Makkah and Madinah — the Haramain High-Speed Rail

The fastest and most comfortable way to cover ground: roughly two and a half hours between Makkah and Madinah, with a stop at Jeddah's King Abdulaziz International Airport along the way. Book through the official HHR Train app or website — enter your passport name exactly as it appears on your passport, and keep a copy of the ticket offline.

2

From the airport to your hotel

Jeddah's King Abdulaziz International is the main gateway for most pilgrims; Madinah's Prince Mohammad bin Abdulaziz International handles many direct Umrah flights too. Careem, Uber and metered taxis all operate from both — use the airport's marked pickup area rather than accepting an offer from someone walking the arrivals hall. Many Umrah packages also include a pre-arranged private transfer; check with your travel agent before booking your own.

3

Around the city and out to ziyarat sites

Official white taxis are metered and available near hotels and the Haram; agree on the fare or insist on the meter before you set off. For a half-day trip to sites like Jabal al-Nour or Uhud, your hotel or a licensed local tour operator can usually arrange a car and driver for a fixed price — often more relaxed than juggling an app for several stops.

4

Moving through the mosque itself

Both Masjid al-Haram and Masjid an-Nabawi are largely a walk once you're there — most hotels within the central zone are five to fifteen minutes on foot. For anyone who needs extra support, the mosque authorities provide free wheelchairs at designated points near the gates, and paid electric-cart services operate along the outer courtyards for longer distances.

A practical habit: keep your hotel's name and address written down in Arabic (ask reception to write it for you), since not every driver reads English signage well, and a language gap is the most common cause of a longer-than-expected ride.

Famous places worth knowing

Beyond the two mosques, both cities hold sites tied closely to the Seerah. Many are optional ziyarat stops rather than rites of Umrah itself.

Masjid al-Haram

The Grand Mosque surrounding the Ka'bah — the direction every Muslim faces in prayer, and the heart of both Umrah and Hajj.

View on map ↗

Jabal al-Nour & Cave of Hira

The mountain where the Prophet ﷺ received the first revelation inside the cave of Hira, reached by a steep climb east of the Haram.

View on map ↗

Jabal Thawr

South of the city, where the Prophet ﷺ and Abu Bakr sheltered in a cave during the migration to Madinah while being pursued.

View on map ↗

Mina

The tented valley where Hajj pilgrims stay for several days — a short distance from the Haram, quiet outside the Hajj season.

View on map ↗

Mount Arafat

Where the Prophet ﷺ gave his Farewell Sermon; standing here is the single obligatory pillar of Hajj, though it lies outside Umrah.

View on map ↗

Muzdalifah

The open plain between Arafat and Mina where Hajj pilgrims gather pebbles and spend the night under open sky.

View on map ↗

Masjid an-Nabawi

The Prophet's ﷺ Mosque in the heart of Madinah, built around his original house and expanded into one of the world's largest mosques.

View on map ↗

Al-Rawdah Al-Sharifah

The garden of Paradise on earth, described in hadith — the space between the Prophet's ﷺ pulpit and his house, now permit-only.

View on map ↗

Quba Mosque

The first mosque in Islamic history, founded by the Prophet ﷺ on arrival in Madinah — praying two rak'ahs here carries the reward of an Umrah, per hadith.

View on map ↗

Masjid al-Qiblatain

The Mosque of the Two Qiblahs, where the direction of prayer changed mid-salah from Jerusalem to Makkah.

View on map ↗

Mount Uhud

Site of the second major battle of early Islam; the martyrs' cemetery at its foot, including Hamzah ibn Abd al-Muttalib, is a common ziyarat stop.

View on map ↗

Jannat al-Baqi

Madinah's historic cemetery beside the Prophet's Mosque, resting place of many of his family and companions.

المتاحف

Museums & heritage sites worth a visit

Beyond the two mosques, both cities have museums covering Islamic history, the Prophet's ﷺ biography, and the region's heritage — a good option for a free half-day, especially with family. Prices and hours are what's publicly listed at the time of writing; both change, so treat them as a starting point and check on arrival or via Siyaha (the official ticketing platform for Makkah's attractions).

Makkah Clock Tower Museum

Spread over several floors of the Clock Royal Tower itself, ending in a 360° viewing terrace directly over the Haram and Ka'bah. Entry: ~SAR 150 (terrace only) to SAR 200 (museum + terrace), SAR 400 for VIP fast-track; children roughly half price. Hours: 2pm–11pm daily. Near: directly above Abraj Al Bait mall, a short walk from King Abdulaziz Gate.

View on map ↗

The Makkah Museum (Qasr Khuzam)

A former royal guesthouse turned museum of Makkah's history, Arabic calligraphy and historical artefacts, with separate prayer halls for men and women. Entry: a modest fee, typically a few riyals — confirm locally. Hours: 8am–12:30pm & 4pm–9pm daily. Near: Makkah–Madinah Road, Al Zahir district, a short taxi ride from the Haram.

View on map ↗

International Exhibition of the Prophet's Biography

A large, modern exhibition on the Prophet's ﷺ life and the spread of Islam, popular with families and school groups. Entry: ~SAR 70–105 per adult. Hours: vary by season, typically mid-morning to late evening. Near: Hira Cultural District, a short drive from the Haram.

View on map ↗

Holy Qur'an & Revelation Exhibition

A focused exhibition on the history of Qur'anic manuscripts and the revelation, part of the wider Hira Cultural District. Entry: ~SAR 30–35. Hours: typically 2 hours visit, check same-day timing on arrival. Near: Hira Cultural District, near Jabal al-Nour.

View on map ↗

Bait Al-Aseel Heritage Museum & Museum of Ancient Crafts

Two small, inexpensive heritage stops showing old Hijazi household life and traditional crafts — a quick, budget-friendly add-on rather than a half-day trip. Entry: ~SAR 10–20 each. Near: central Makkah, within easy reach of the Haram.

View on map ↗

Dar Al-Madinah Museum

Madinah's main heritage museum — miniature models recreating the Prophet's ﷺ time and the mosque's historical expansions, inside the old Hijaz Railway station grounds. Entry: ~SAR 25 adult, SAR 15 child. Hours: roughly 9am–5pm, varies by day. Near: Knowledge Economic City, close to Madinah's airport and the Haramain railway station — a taxi ride from the mosque, not walking distance.

View on map ↗

Hejaz Railway Museum

The restored Ottoman-era railway station that once linked Damascus to Madinah — 19 galleries plus original train carriages, some turned into cafés. Entry: free. Hours: 9am–9pm (9am–4pm & 5pm–9pm on Fridays). Near: about 1km from Masjid an-Nabawi — walkable for most visitors.

View on map ↗

International Exhibition of the Prophet's Biography — Madinah

Madinah's branch of the same Seerah exhibition found in Makkah, telling the story of the migration (Hijrah) and the early Muslim community in the city. Entry: ~SAR 40–90 depending on ticket type. Near: within the Haramain expansion zone, a short walk or short ride from the mosque.

View on map ↗

أبواب الحرمين

Key gates of the two mosques, and what's near each

Both mosques have well over a hundred numbered gates, but pilgrims mostly orient themselves by a handful of named ones. Knowing which gate is near your hotel — or near Safa, the Rawdah, or Jannat al-Baqi — saves a lot of walking around the outside wall. Gate numbers shift slightly as the mosques keep expanding, so treat names as the reliable part and numbers as a rough guide.

King Abdulaziz Gate (Bab al-Malik) — Gate 1

The busiest gate, opening directly onto the Mataf. Near: the Clock Tower (Abraj Al Bait) and Safwa Towers — the default gate for most Clock Tower-area hotels, so expect queues at peak times.

King Fahd Gate — Gate 79

A wide southern-side entrance, generally calmer than King Abdulaziz Gate. Near: the Ajyad district — a good alternative if your hotel is a short walk from either side.

King Abdullah Gate — Gate 100

One of the newest and largest gates, on the mosque's northeastern side. Near: the Al-Shamiyyah expansion and its newer hotel towers.

Umrah Gate (Bab Umrah) — Gate 63

An easy-to-remember name on the Shami (Syrian) corner side, commonly used by Umrah pilgrims specifically. Near: the Shamiyyah side, opposite King Abdullah Gate's general area.

As-Salam Gate (Bab as-Salam)

Historically the gate first-time visitors are encouraged to enter through, sitting between Safa and Marwah (closer to Marwah) and opening straight onto the mas'aa. Near: the Safa–Marwah gallery, so useful both for your first entry and for starting Sa'i.

Safa Gate (Bab as-Safa) — Gate 11

Opens right at the base of Safa hill. Near: the start of the Sa'i walk — the gate to look for once you're ready to begin Sa'i after Tawaf.

Bab as-Salam (Gate of Peace) — Gate 1

The most historic gate, traditionally used to enter and greet the Prophet ﷺ, leading toward the Rawdah. Near: the western side — close to Anwar Al Madinah Mövenpick and Dar Al Taqwa; a common choice for men heading to the Rawdah.

Bab Jibril (Gate of Gabriel)

On the eastern side, near the Prophet's ﷺ chamber and mihrab. Near: Dallah Taibah and Al Aqeeq hotels — also commonly used for Rawdah access.

King Fahd Gate (Bab Malik Fahd)

The largest gate at Masjid an-Nabawi — seven doors and two minarets — forming the main northern entrance. Near: the northern hotel zone and the main plaza where crowds gather before Fajr.

Bab Al-Majeedi

A large northern gate near King Abdul Majeed Road. Near: Madinah Hilton, InterContinental Dar Al Iman and Millennium Taiba — the gate most of that hotel cluster uses.

Bab Al-Baqi

Faces Jannat al-Baqi directly. Near: the most convenient gate if you're stepping out to Baqi after a prayer rather than planning a separate trip.

Bab An-Nisa (Women's Gate)

A dedicated women's entrance, with several more added across the mosque's expansions. Near: look for "النساء" (women) signage — staff can point to the nearest one from any side of the mosque.

A practical habit: note your gate's name (and a nearby landmark) when you arrive each time, not just "the big one near my hotel" — several gates can look similar from inside, and this is the detail that gets you back out to the right street fastest.

Medical care and emergencies

Every Umrah visa issued from outside Saudi Arabia comes with mandatory health insurance already built into the visa fee — here's what it covers, where to get treated, and who to call.

Included in your visa

What the mandatory insurance covers

  • Emergency medical treatment and hospitalisation, up to SAR 100,000
  • COVID-19 diagnosis and treatment
  • Pregnancy complications and emergency childbirth
  • Emergency dental care and dialysis
  • Traffic accident injuries
  • Medical evacuation, and repatriation in the event of death
  • Compensation for flight delays or cancellations
  • Valid for 90 days from your entry date, within Saudi Arabia only
Using it

How to access care

  • Government hospitals and affiliated private facilities both accept it — bring your passport and visa/Nusuk details
  • Verify your policy anytime at riaya-ksa.com, the Comprehensive Insurance Programme for the Guests of the Merciful
  • Policy helpline: 800 440 0008 (within Saudi Arabia) or +966 13 812 9700 (international)
  • For anything the policy doesn't cover — routine consultations, non-emergency specialist visits — private hospitals are an option at your own cost
✚
Near Masjid al-Haram

Makkah

  • Ajyad Emergency Hospital — 24-hour emergency care, closest to the Haram itself
  • Al-Haram Emergency Hospital — just north of the mosque, opposite King Abdullah Gate
  • Al-Noor Specialist Hospital — around 3.5 km out, for specialist and non-emergency care
  • King Abdullah Medical City — the region's larger tertiary hospital
✚
Near Masjid an-Nabawi

Madinah

  • First-aid points sit right in the mosque's own courtyards, near the Bab Jibril, Al-Safiyyah, Bab As-Salam and Al-Majidi gates — the fastest option for anything minor
  • King Fahad Hospital — the region's largest and most comprehensive facility
  • Al-Ansar Hospital — a further major government hospital serving the city
